# Stage 2 cross-book synthesis

Inputs: the nine verified Stage 1 files in `data/stage1/` (137 insights: 6 High, 113 Medium, 18 Low confidence). Taxonomy: `docs/taxonomy.md`. Companion machine-readable file: `data/learning-path.json`.

This memo synthesizes what the nine books already contain. It adds no new research. Where it mentions evidence, it relies on the `underlying_research`, `confidence_rationale` and `flags` fields written and verified in Stage 1. Where it names candidate sources for Stage 3, those are suggestions for where to look, not claims about what those sources say.

Book ids used below: `communicate-with-mastery` (Schramm and Levy), `connect` (Bradford and Robin), `how-to-know-a-person` (David Brooks), `humor-seriously` (Aaker and Bagdonas), `improv-wisdom` (Madson), `made-to-stick` (Heath and Heath), `supercommunicators` (Duhigg), `talk-wood-brooks` (Alison Wood Brooks), `think-faster-talk-smarter` (Abrahams).

## 1. Executive summary

The nine books agree far more than they disagree. Across them, a small set of ideas recurs so often that it can serve as the backbone of the guide:

1. **Attention goes outward.** Nearly every book says the same thing in different words: stop monitoring yourself and attend to the other person. Madson calls it paying attention, David Brooks calls it being a loud listener, Abrahams calls it listening before you leap, Duhigg calls it proving you are listening. The best-supported version is `improv-wisdom-06` (High).
2. **Ask, and especially follow up.** Follow-up questions, deep questions and perspective getting (asking rather than guessing) appear in five books. Two of the six High-confidence insights (`how-to-know-a-person-04`, `supercommunicators-05`) and a third (`how-to-know-a-person-06`) belong to this cluster.
3. **Our social fears are miscalibrated.** A striking research convergence: people overestimate how awkward deep talk, sensitive questions, compliments and visible vulnerability will be, and overestimate how clearly their own tone and meaning come across. This one idea links the Epley-lab findings cited by Brooks, Duhigg and Wood Brooks with the curse of knowledge in Made to Stick and the spotlight effect cited by Madson and Abrahams.
4. **Connection over perfection.** Abrahams, Madson and Schramm (with a direct line of influence between them) all tell the reader to lower the bar, treat mistakes as offers, and practice rather than perform.
5. **Structure frees the mind.** Short reusable shapes (What, So What, Now What; Problem, Solution, Benefit; ADD; the 4 I's; AAA) appear across Abrahams and Schramm, and Made to Stick and Schramm agree on leading with the core point.
6. **Separate behavior and impact from intent.** Connect's three realities and net, the 4 I's, David Brooks's perspective-getting argument and Wood Brooks's layers all say: describe what you saw and how it affected you, and ask rather than assert what the other person meant.
7. **Match the kind of conversation.** Duhigg (three kinds), Wood Brooks (compass and layers), David Brooks (official versus actual conversation) and Connect (inquiry before advice) converge on diagnosing what the other person needs before you respond.

The main tensions are about **dosage, not direction**: how much to prepare (Madson versus Wood Brooks and Abrahams), how much humor (Aaker and Bagdonas versus their own cautions and Duhigg's online advice), how fast and how far to disclose (Connect's 15 percent stretch versus Duhigg's and David Brooks's push toward depth), and when to apologize (Wood Brooks's superfluous apologies versus the advice from three books not to apologize for your own stumbles). Each resolves once the reader distinguishes the target of the advice (purpose versus script; harm to others versus your own performance; core competence versus feelings).

On evidence, the library is solid but narrower than it looks. Only six insights are High, and the strongest research clusters come from a few labs (Epley at Chicago; Wood Brooks and colleagues at Harvard; Minson; the Dartmouth synchrony group). Several "convergences" are partly lineage rather than independent confirmation (Abrahams credits Madson; Schramm credits Abrahams and Made to Stick; Humor, Seriously and Talk both rest on the same Bitterly, Brooks and Schweitzer humor paper). Named frameworks across all nine books are practitioner heuristics that have not been tested as packages. Gino-coauthored papers underpin three insights directly and several more by mapping; per the user decision they stay in, with an integrity note.

Coverage is strongest in relationships, foundations and conversation skills, and weakest in written and digital communication (async has zero insights), influence via reciprocity, saying no, virtual presenting, introductions and proposals or memos. Professional and personal-life categories are almost always secondary tags rather than the main subject of an insight (3 and 2 primary tags respectively), so the library teaches techniques and leaves application to situations thin. Stage 3 should prioritize research-based sources on writing for busy readers and async work, a reciprocity and persuasion source, an assertiveness and saying-no source, a virtual communication source, and a couples or family communication source grounded in research.

The proposed learning path has seven stages and 92 core insights: Foundations (mindset, nerves, purpose), Listening and understanding, Conversation and connection, Clarity and structure in speech and writing, Influence and memorable messages, Difficult situations, and Mastery in groups and practice.

## 2. Convergent concepts

Ordered roughly by combined strength and breadth. "Books" counts distinct books. Evidence ratings combine the Stage 1 ratings with how independent the sources are.

### 2.1 Outward attention beats self-monitoring
- **Insight ids:** `improv-wisdom-06`, `improv-wisdom-14`, `how-to-know-a-person-03`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-06`, `how-to-know-a-person-01`, `communicate-with-mastery-06`, `supercommunicators-13` (4 books plus Duhigg's framing)
- **Agreement:** Attention focused on yourself (how am I doing, what will I say next) blocks both listening and fluent speaking. Shifting focus to the other person improves the interaction and, as a side effect, reduces anxiety.
- **Differences:** Madson treats attention as a contemplative practice (notice something new, sweep distracting thoughts aside like a windshield wiper). David Brooks emphasizes making attention visible, a switch not a dimmer, with an active face and follow-up questions. Abrahams frames it as a pressure skill (pace, space, grace, then listen for the bottom line), partly to buy time. Schramm applies it to presenting (one thought to one person). Duhigg offers a neural-synchrony picture that the Stage 1 file flags as pop-neuroscience overreach.
- **Evidence:** High for the core claim. `improv-wisdom-06` is rated High on independent listening research (Itzchakov, Kluger and Castro) and the clinical model of self-focused attention in social anxiety. The specific exercises (windshield wipers, SLANT, four to five seconds of eye contact) are untested.

### 2.2 Ask more questions, especially follow-ups
- **Insight ids:** `talk-wood-brooks-04`, `talk-wood-brooks-05`, `talk-wood-brooks-06`, `supercommunicators-06`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-13`, `connect-09`, `how-to-know-a-person-01`, `how-to-know-a-person-03` (5 books)
- **Agreement:** People ask too few questions. Follow-ups that build on what was just said signal listening and increase liking. Do not turn a question into a launch pad for yourself (Wood Brooks's boomerasking; David Brooks's topping in `how-to-know-a-person-08`; Abrahams's warning about turning answers back to yourself).
- **Differences:** Wood Brooks offers a taxonomy (introductory, mirror, full-switch, follow-up) and warns against both too few follow-ups and too many switches. Duhigg's version is about groups: socially central people speak less and ask far more. Connect stresses open how, what, when, where questions and avoiding why. Abrahams packages it as "be interested, not interesting" (credited to Rachel Greenwald).
- **Evidence:** Medium. The main study (Huang et al., 2017) shows modest effects (d about 0.3), its speed-dating link was challenged by Kluger and Malloy (2019), and it is Gino-coauthored (see section 4). The Sievers et al. (2024) group study adds independent support for question-asking in groups. Boomerasking rests on one recent multi-study paper from Wood Brooks's own team.

### 2.3 Go deeper than feels comfortable: depth is less awkward than you fear
- **Insight ids:** `how-to-know-a-person-04`, `how-to-know-a-person-05`, `how-to-know-a-person-09`, `supercommunicators-05`, `talk-wood-brooks-03`, `talk-wood-brooks-07` (3 books)
- **Agreement:** People avoid deep or sensitive questions because they expect awkwardness and rejection, and they are usually wrong. Questions about values, turning points and stories build connection.
- **Differences:** David Brooks pushes big, open crossroads questions and asking for the story rather than the summary. Duhigg emphasizes that deep questions can be light (did you always want to be a lawyer?) and must be reciprocal (Fast Friends works only when partners alternate). Wood Brooks offers the most graduated model: a topic pyramid from small talk to tailored to deep, with the explicit note that not every conversation needs to reach the top, and that small talk is a necessary ritual. She also adds that sensitive questions cost less than feared but should be asked privately and with an easy out.
- **Evidence:** High. Kardas, Kumar and Epley (2022) and the Aron et al. (1997) procedure are well supported; Hart, VanEpps and Schweitzer (2021) supports the sensitive-questions point (one paper). The topic-pyramid and switching data come from Brooks-lab studies not located in peer-reviewed form.

### 2.4 Social fears and self-perceptions are systematically miscalibrated
- **Insight ids:** `how-to-know-a-person-04`, `talk-wood-brooks-07`, `talk-wood-brooks-10`, `connect-10`, `improv-wisdom-05`, `improv-wisdom-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-02`, `communicate-with-mastery-09`, `supercommunicators-12`, `talk-wood-brooks-11` (7 books)
- **Agreement:** This is a cross-cutting finding that no single book names but the library supports strongly. We overestimate how awkward depth, sensitive questions and compliments will be; we overestimate how much others notice our stumbles (the spotlight effect); we underestimate how positively others see our vulnerability (the beautiful mess effect, mapped in `connect-10`); we overestimate how well our tone comes across in email (Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng, 2005, mapped in `communicate-with-mastery-09` and `supercommunicators-12`); and we misjudge when a partner wants a conversation to end (Mastroianni et al., 2021).
- **Differences:** The books use the finding for different ends: as encouragement to act (Wood Brooks, David Brooks, Madson, Abrahams) or as a warning to check and verify (Schramm, Duhigg on text).
- **Evidence:** High as a family of findings. Several individual papers come from one lab (Epley and colleagues), and some links to the books are mapping rather than confirmed citation; but the direction is consistent across independent paradigms.

### 2.5 Ask rather than infer: humility about reading other minds
- **Insight ids:** `how-to-know-a-person-06`, `how-to-know-a-person-14`, `how-to-know-a-person-11`, `connect-03`, `connect-09`, `supercommunicators-03`, `made-to-stick-01` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** You know less about what others think, feel and intend than you believe. Connect says you cannot see across the net into another's intentions; David Brooks cites empathic-accuracy research showing modest accuracy and overconfidence; Duhigg's learning conversation rule is to ask what others are looking for; Made to Stick shows the mirror problem, that you cannot unknow what you know.
- **Differences:** David Brooks's own empathy model (`how-to-know-a-person-11`) describes mentalizing as projecting from your own experience, which sits uneasily with the perspective-getting research the Stage 1 file attaches to his book. Wood Brooks's compass (`talk-wood-brooks-12`) asks you to plot your best guess at your partner's goals. See tension 3.5.
- **Evidence:** High for the research core (Eyal, Steffel and Epley, 2018, 25 experiments), but note that `how-to-know-a-person-06` carries the `not-confirmed-in-book` flag. Empathic-accuracy percentages are task-dependent (`contested`).

### 2.6 Looping and paraphrasing for understanding
- **Insight ids:** `how-to-know-a-person-07`, `supercommunicators-07`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-06`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-11`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-16`, `supercommunicators-11`, `communicate-with-mastery-09` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Restate what you heard in your own words and check whether you got it right. This both catches misunderstanding and shows the other person they were heard, which lowers defensiveness.
- **Differences:** Duhigg and David Brooks describe the full loop (repeat until confirmed) and both warn against parroting; Brooks notes a lighter paraphrase often works. Abrahams uses paraphrase strategically as well: to buy thinking time, to reward a questioner, and to reframe a hostile question in Q&A. In conflict he adds "acknowledge before advocating" and using "and" not "but". Schramm's "verify" step applies the same idea to written messages.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Widely used in mediation (Friedman and Himmelstein's loop of understanding) and consistent with listening research, but Weger and colleagues (2014) found paraphrasing added little over simple acknowledgment in some conditions, and Duhigg's own case (the gun dialogue) showed people felt heard but rarely changed views.

### 2.7 Connection over perfection
- **Insight ids:** `think-faster-talk-smarter-04`, `improv-wisdom-05`, `improv-wisdom-10`, `improv-wisdom-04`, `communicate-with-mastery-10`, `communicate-with-mastery-17`, `humor-seriously-01` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Striving to be impressive makes people stiff, slow and self-focused. Aim for an ordinary, adequate contribution, treat mistakes as offers or missed takes, recover lightly and keep going.
- **Differences:** Madson frames it as a life philosophy (be average, make mistakes please). Abrahams adapts it explicitly from Madson for spontaneous speaking ("maximize mediocrity", "dare to be dull") and limits it to in-the-moment delivery, not planned communication. Schramm frames it as long-run mastery: no perfect talk exists, so keep improving through reflection. Aaker and Bagdonas apply it to humor: a modest attempt at lightness beats a polished joke.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Consistent with perfectionism research and the spotlight effect, but no direct test of "lower your standard" instructions. Note the lineage: Abrahams credits Madson, so this is less independent than four books suggest.

### 2.8 Reframe anxiety rather than suppress it
- **Insight ids:** `think-faster-talk-smarter-03`, `communicate-with-mastery-19`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-02`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-05`, `improv-wisdom-03` (3 books)
- **Agreement:** You do not need to eliminate nerves. Manage them to a workable level and point the arousal somewhere useful.
- **Differences in mechanism:** Abrahams offers the fullest toolkit: calm the body first (long exhale breathing), then relabel nerves as excitement, then redefine the moment as opportunity rather than threat. Schramm uses the same excitement reframe but pairs it with power posing, which the Stage 1 file marks as failed to replicate. Madson rejects the idea of fixing feelings first: feelings are not controllable on demand, so act while nervous (show up, act now). Wood Brooks wrote the original reappraisal paper, but the Stage 1 verifier could not confirm it appears in Talk, so it is not part of that book's insights.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Brooks (2014) is well known, but a later replication reproduced the felt-excitement effect more clearly than the performance effect (`contested`). Slow breathing and challenge-versus-threat appraisal have reasonable support. Power posing should be dropped or explicitly flagged in the final library.

### 2.9 Structure frees working memory: What, So What, Now What and its relatives
- **Insight ids:** `think-faster-talk-smarter-07`, `communicate-with-mastery-04` (exact duplicate); related structures `think-faster-talk-smarter-08`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-11`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-14`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-15`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-12`, `communicate-with-mastery-18`, `communicate-with-mastery-07`, `communicate-with-mastery-16`, `talk-wood-brooks-01`, `supercommunicators-03` (5 books use named structures)
- **Agreement:** A short, reusable shape makes a message easier to deliver under pressure and easier for the listener to follow. The best structures end with relevance to the listener and a next step.
- **Differences:** Abrahams treats What, So What, Now What as a spontaneous speaking aid and drills it as a habit; Schramm places it in the writing chapter as "synthesis over summary". Abrahams supplies a structure for nearly every situation (pitch, Q&A, toast, feedback, apology, conflict); Madson's `improv-wisdom-13` offers a looser alternative (questions to yourself instead of a script). Wood Brooks and Duhigg offer structures for conversation (TALK, four rules) rather than for messages.
- **Evidence:** Medium. All named structures are practitioner heuristics; none has been tested as a named framework. The general claim that organized messages are easier to process and recall is well accepted. The What, So What, Now What form traces to reflective-practice models (Borton; Rolfe and colleagues).

### 2.10 Lead with the core point and cut the preamble
- **Insight ids:** `made-to-stick-03`, `communicate-with-mastery-05`, `communicate-with-mastery-03`, `communicate-with-mastery-02`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-09`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-11`, `improv-wisdom-04` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Find the single most important thing, say it first, and drop background the audience does not need. Made to Stick calls it finding the core (Commander's Intent); Schramm calls it parachuting in; Abrahams calls it focus ("they want the time, not how the clock was built") and the first A in ADD (answer in one sentence).
- **Differences:** Made to Stick stresses ruthless prioritization ("if you say three things, you say nothing"); Schramm stresses opening at the most interesting moment for engagement; Madson says start with the obvious, which is closer to "do not wait for brilliance" than to prioritization.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Strong professional-writing consensus; book support is case-based (the Southwest salad example is a single, secondhand anecdote).

### 2.11 Curse of knowledge and audience-first planning
- **Insight ids:** `made-to-stick-01`, `communicate-with-mastery-01`, `communicate-with-mastery-16`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-09`, `made-to-stick-10`, `improv-wisdom-08`, `talk-wood-brooks-12` (5 books)
- **Agreement:** Start from the audience: what they know, care about and need to do. Experts cannot easily imagine not knowing, so clarity felt by the speaker is unreliable and must be checked with concrete examples and outsiders.
- **Differences:** Schramm's AIM orders the work (audience, intent, then message) and scales up to a nine-box canvas for high-stakes messages. Made to Stick diagnoses the cause (the curse of knowledge) and prescribes stickiness tools. Abrahams compresses it into a quick in-the-moment check (precise, relevant, accessible, concise). Madson and Wood Brooks focus on purpose and goals in conversation rather than on audience knowledge.
- **Evidence:** Medium. The broad phenomenon is well supported, but the tapping study is an unpublished dissertation and a high-powered replication of a related paradigm found the effect less than half the original size (`unreplicated`). Audience analysis itself is long-standing consensus.

### 2.12 Disclosure and vulnerability build trust, when reciprocal and calibrated
- **Insight ids:** `connect-02`, `connect-10`, `connect-01`, `supercommunicators-08`, `supercommunicators-05`, `communicate-with-mastery-08`, `humor-seriously-05`, `humor-seriously-11`, `how-to-know-a-person-02` (5 books)
- **Agreement:** Sharing feelings and uncertainties, and receiving the other person's sharing in return, is how relationships deepen. Disclosure should leave room for reciprocity, and it should not undermine your core competence (stated independently by Connect in `connect-10` and by Aaker and Bagdonas in `humor-seriously-05`).
- **Differences:** Connect prescribes gradual steps (stretch about 15 percent past comfort, then watch the response) and says the higher-status person should go first. Duhigg argues the sequence often runs vulnerability first, trust second, while noting a junior person bears more risk. Schramm focuses on purposeful disclosure to an audience (plan the start and end, serve the listener). David Brooks's accompaniment counsels patience and warns against pushing for disclosure too soon. Aaker and Bagdonas add shared laughter as a disclosure accelerant. See tension 3.3.
- **Evidence:** Medium to High. Reciprocal, gradual disclosure building closeness is well replicated (Collins and Miller meta-analysis and Aron et al. procedure, mostly mapped rather than confirmed as cited). The 15 percent figure is a teaching heuristic (`pop-psychology`); the laughter-disclosure study showed effects only in observer ratings.

### 2.13 Match the kind of conversation before you respond
- **Insight ids:** `supercommunicators-01`, `supercommunicators-02`, `supercommunicators-03`, `talk-wood-brooks-12`, `talk-wood-brooks-14`, `how-to-know-a-person-12`, `connect-09`, `how-to-know-a-person-10` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Many misfires happen because two people are in different conversations: one solving a problem, the other wanting to be understood, or a surface topic masking a feeling or identity issue. Diagnose first, then meet the other person there.
- **Differences:** Duhigg's typology is three kinds (practical, emotional, social) plus matching mood and energy. Wood Brooks offers two tools: a goals compass (informational versus relational) and five layers (content, emotions, beliefs, motives, identity). David Brooks distinguishes the official and actual conversation. Connect and David Brooks apply the idea to support: inquiry and presence before advice.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Each typology is the author's synthesis and untested as a package; all overlap with the Difficult Conversations framework (what happened, feelings, identity). The neural-alignment research behind matching is correlational.

### 2.14 Feedback: describe behavior and impact, never motive or personality
- **Insight ids:** `connect-03`, `connect-04`, `connect-06`, `connect-15`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-10`, `made-to-stick-07`, `communicate-with-mastery-15` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Name the specific, observable behavior and its effect, avoid labels and guesses about intent, and ask for a change in behavior, not in character. Concrete feedback is clearer and easier to act on.
- **Differences:** Connect builds the whole model around the net and real feelings (not "I feel that"), and rejects the feedback sandwich. Abrahams's 4 I's adds an explicit Invitation and Implications (consequences), which leans more directive than Connect's "data, not a verdict" stance (see tension 3.7). Schramm adds Radical Candor and coaching rather than rewriting someone's work.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Supported by feedback-intervention research (Kluger and DeNisi, 1996) favoring task and behavior focus, and by affect-labeling research for the feelings piece. The named models are untested.

### 2.15 A learning stance in disagreement: understand before you advocate
- **Insight ids:** `talk-wood-brooks-09`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-16`, `supercommunicators-11`, `supercommunicators-03`, `connect-08`, `how-to-know-a-person-08`, `how-to-know-a-person-12`, `talk-wood-brooks-14` (5 books)
- **Agreement:** In conflict or feedback, aim to learn rather than win. Acknowledge the other view, look for the real layer of difference, find points of agreement, and treat the exchange as joint problem solving. Paradoxically, this is often more persuasive.
- **Differences:** Wood Brooks gives a linguistic recipe (acknowledge, hedge, avoid "because" and "therefore", frame positively). Abrahams's CLEAR begins with checking yourself and draws on Minson and Crucial Conversations. Duhigg measures success by whether people feel heard and keep talking. David Brooks emphasizes conditions before content (safety first) and finding the shared value. Connect stresses that the giver of feedback also lacks part of the picture.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Conversational receptiveness research is multi-study and peer-reviewed but Gino-coauthored (two papers); Ren and Schaumberg (2024) is independent support for the "disagreement is mistaken for bad listening" point. Duhigg's cases are narrative.

### 2.16 Humor: lightness over jokes, with asymmetric risk
- **Insight ids:** `humor-seriously-01`, `humor-seriously-03`, `humor-seriously-04`, `humor-seriously-09`, `humor-seriously-11`, `talk-wood-brooks-08`, `improv-wisdom-12`, `supercommunicators-09` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Humor is mostly a mindset of noticing what is true and being willing to share it lightly. Successful humor raises perceived confidence; a mild flop costs little; inappropriate humor costs a lot. Laughing at others' humor and callbacks count as much as telling jokes. Laughter is a bonding signal.
- **Differences:** Aaker and Bagdonas argue humor is badly underused at work and provide craft (truth plus misdirection) and safety checks (truth, pain, distance; punch up). Wood Brooks treats levity as a cure for boredom and advises low-risk lightness. Madson frames play as loosening the mind. Duhigg uses laughter as an emotional signal to read and match.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Note that Humor, Seriously and Talk both rest on the same paper (Bitterly, Brooks and Schweitzer, 2017; Wood Brooks is a coauthor), so this is one research line, not two. The humor cliff (`humor-seriously-02`) and four humor styles (`humor-seriously-07`) are Low. The book's authors have an incentive to emphasize benefits.

### 2.17 Make stories and specifics do the work
- **Insight ids:** `made-to-stick-07`, `made-to-stick-12`, `made-to-stick-14`, `made-to-stick-04`, `communicate-with-mastery-07`, `communicate-with-mastery-05`, `humor-seriously-08`, `humor-seriously-16`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-11`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-15`, `how-to-know-a-person-09` (5 books)
- **Agreement:** Concrete details, examples, analogies, human-scale numbers and short stories make messages understood and remembered. Specificity also makes humor work and makes answers credible (Abrahams's "detail" and "for example").
- **Differences:** Made to Stick is the theoretical anchor; Schramm offers a story template (story spine, `Low`, not confirmed in book); Abrahams embeds a concrete example into every structure; David Brooks turns it around for listening, asking others for their story.
- **Evidence:** High for concreteness (`made-to-stick-07`, one of the most robust memory findings). Medium for stories (process simulation evidence is good; the stories-versus-statistics classroom figures are informal and often inflated). Human-scale numbers have independent randomized support (Barrio et al., 2016).

### 2.18 Persuade through the listener's benefit
- **Insight ids:** `made-to-stick-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-08`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-15`, `communicate-with-mastery-18`, `communicate-with-mastery-01`, `supercommunicators-04` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Start from a problem the listener recognizes, and help them picture the benefit to them rather than listing features. Ask for action at the end.
- **Differences:** Made to Stick adds that people care about more than money (avoid Maslow's basement) and about imagery of themselves benefiting. Abrahams and Schramm package it as pitch structures. Duhigg distinguishes cost-benefit logic (practical conversations) from similarity logic (emotional conversations).
- **Evidence:** Medium. Strong persuasion consensus; the imagery study is a single 1982 experiment, and the pitch structures are practitioner forms.

### 2.19 Identity shapes what people hear
- **Insight ids:** `supercommunicators-10`, `supercommunicators-14`, `supercommunicators-01`, `made-to-stick-11`, `talk-wood-brooks-14`, `communicate-with-mastery-14` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** People process messages through who they are and the groups they belong to. When a message threatens identity, more facts can harden resistance; connecting to a valued or shared identity opens people up.
- **Differences:** Made to Stick uses identity as a persuasion lever (what would someone like me do?). Duhigg uses it to defuse resistance and make hard conversations safer (shared identities, norms). Wood Brooks names identity as one layer where difference can hide. Schramm applies it to the speaker's own identity (clarity over conformity, Low).
- **Evidence:** Medium. Social identity effects are well studied and Duhigg cites a randomized field experiment (Mousa, 2020); the Texas campaign and physician cases are anecdotes.

### 2.20 Apologize specifically and own it
- **Insight ids:** `think-faster-talk-smarter-12`, `talk-wood-brooks-13`, `humor-seriously-10`, `connect-14` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** A real apology names the specific act, acknowledges its effect on the other person, avoids "but" and "if you were offended", and commits to change. Relationships can recover from serious ruptures, and the repair can make them stronger.
- **Differences:** Abrahams gives a three-part form (acknowledge, appreciate, amends). Wood Brooks adds a surprising finding: superfluous apologies for things you did not cause signal empathy and raise trust. Aaker and Bagdonas apply it to a joke that crossed a line. Connect focuses on the longer arc of repair with curiosity rather than blame. See tension 3.4.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Apology components have research support (Lewicki and colleagues, mapped); the superfluous-apology paper is one paper from the author's team; Connect's repair story is a single anecdote.

### 2.21 Express appreciation more than feels necessary
- **Insight ids:** `talk-wood-brooks-10`, `improv-wisdom-09`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-14`, `supercommunicators-12` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Specific thanks and sincere compliments are undergiven and land better than expected. Generic thanks does less.
- **Differences:** Wood Brooks frames it as correcting a miscalibration; Madson as a reflective habit (Naikan questions); Abrahams builds thanks into the toast structure; Duhigg prescribes extra gratitude online.
- **Evidence:** High for the core (Zhao and Epley, 2021; Kumar and Epley, 2018, the latter mapped). Naikan itself is thinly studied.

### 2.22 Raise small issues early and set expectations explicitly
- **Insight ids:** `connect-05`, `connect-13`, `connect-12`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-10`, `supercommunicators-14` (3 books)
- **Agreement:** Unspoken irritations and mismatched expectations accumulate into bigger blowups. Raising them early, in non-accusatory language, and making norms explicit keeps stakes low.
- **Differences:** Connect supplies the core models (pinches and crunches; boundaries that keep you close; balancing influence). Abrahams stresses in-the-moment timing. Duhigg applies it at group level (set norms before the hardest conversations).
- **Evidence:** Medium. Strong practitioner consensus; the pinch-crunch model originates in organization development (Sherwood and Glidewell) and is not experimentally validated. This concept is mostly one book, which is why boundaries and saying no are flagged as gaps.

### 2.23 Elevate others in groups; dominance lowers shared understanding
- **Insight ids:** `improv-wisdom-11`, `improv-wisdom-01`, `supercommunicators-06`, `supercommunicators-13`, `humor-seriously-14`, `humor-seriously-04`, `how-to-know-a-person-08` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** In groups, the most effective people make others look good, accept and build on offers, ask rather than hold the floor, and laugh at others' jokes. Dominant talkers reduce alignment.
- **Differences:** Madson grounds it in improv ethics; Duhigg in the Dartmouth neural-alignment study; Aaker and Bagdonas in leaders making room for others' humor and status-aware joking; David Brooks in the midwife model.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Sievers et al. (2024) and psychological safety research (Edmondson, mapped) give indirect support; Lehmann-Willenbrock and Allen (2014) supports back-and-forth humor patterns in teams. Specific tactics are untested.

### 2.24 Supporting someone in distress: presence and inquiry before advice
- **Insight ids:** `connect-09`, `how-to-know-a-person-10`, `how-to-know-a-person-02`, `supercommunicators-02`, `supercommunicators-01` (3 books)
- **Agreement:** When someone is struggling, resist the urge to fix. Ask open questions, acknowledge how hard it is, and stay present. Advice often serves the giver's discomfort.
- **Differences:** David Brooks's case is clinical depression and stresses many small touches and taking risk seriously alongside professional care; Connect's is everyday problems; Duhigg frames it as matching an emotional conversation.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Consistent with mainstream supporter guidance, but `how-to-know-a-person-10` rests on one personal case and the Duhigg matching cases are anecdotal.

### 2.25 Practice, record and reflect
- **Insight ids:** `communicate-with-mastery-10`, `communicate-with-mastery-15`, `communicate-with-mastery-20`, `communicate-with-mastery-09`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-07`, `talk-wood-brooks-01`, `talk-wood-brooks-07`, `connect-07` (4 books)
- **Agreement:** Communication improves through feedback-driven practice: debrief conversations, record and review yourself, rehearse hard questions with a partner, invite frequent small feedback.
- **Differences:** Schramm is the most developed (four-pass video review, coaching then group rehearsal). Abrahams prescribes drills; Wood Brooks uses TALK as a pre and post checklist; Connect frames feedback as data you need because you cannot see your own impact.
- **Evidence:** Medium. Deliberate practice and feedback research are well supported in general (mapped); the specific routines are practitioner advice.

### 2.26 Know when to move on or end
- **Insight ids:** `talk-wood-brooks-11`, `talk-wood-brooks-03`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-13` (2 books)
- **Agreement:** People stay too long on stale topics and in conversations. Watch for fading energy, switch or close warmly, and exit together rather than abruptly.
- **Differences:** Wood Brooks gives the evidence and the warning signs; Abrahams supplies an exit technique (Greenwald's white flag).
- **Evidence:** Medium. Mastroianni et al. (2021) is a well-powered paper from an independent lab; switching studies are not located in peer-reviewed form.

## 3. Tensions and disagreements

### 3.1 Prepare versus do not prepare (top tension)
- **Ids:** `improv-wisdom-02`, `improv-wisdom-13`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-04` versus `talk-wood-brooks-02`, `supercommunicators-14`, `communicate-with-mastery-01`, `communicate-with-mastery-16`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-07`, `made-to-stick-03`.
- **The conflict:** Madson's maxim is "don't prepare": scripting pulls you out of the present. Wood Brooks reports that about thirty seconds of topic preparation made conversations flow better and reduced anxiety, and Duhigg relays a similar finding (`supercommunicators-04`). Schramm's whole method is structured preparation, and Abrahams gives a ready-made structure for every situation.
- **Reconciliation:** The disagreement is about the object of preparation. Prepare purpose (AIM, stay on course), material (topics, examples, a structure) and, for high stakes, the conditions of the conversation. Do not prepare exact lines or try to anticipate every reply in live interaction. Madson herself disclaims the literal slogan in interviews ("prepare as much as you like, then set it aside"), and Abrahams explicitly limits "maximize mediocrity" to in-the-moment delivery. A good synthesis rule: prepare the skeleton, improvise the words. Wood Brooks's own caution (do not march through the topic list; follow the partner's energy) closes the gap from the other side.

### 3.2 Humor's benefits versus its risks (top tension)
- **Ids:** `humor-seriously-03`, `humor-seriously-11`, `humor-seriously-12`, `humor-seriously-14`, `humor-seriously-16` versus `humor-seriously-06`, `humor-seriously-04`, `humor-seriously-09`, `humor-seriously-05`, `supercommunicators-12`, `humor-seriously-13`, `improv-wisdom-12`.
- **The conflict:** Aaker and Bagdonas argue humor is underused and boosts status, trust, disclosure, negotiation outcomes and memory. Yet the same research line shows inappropriate humor lowers status, a joke attached to a disclosure makes it less believable, and humor fades as a benefit when people fear for their jobs. Duhigg tells people to avoid sarcasm and dry humor online; Humor, Seriously agrees about sarcasm but encourages small levity in writing.
- **Reconciliation:** Use humor for connection and memorability, not for content that must be taken literally. Do not joke when delivering bad news, flagging a risk or setting a boundary (`humor-seriously-06`). Prefer low-risk levity (laughing at others, callbacks, gentle self-deprecation about non-core traits) over ambitious jokes. Run the truth, pain, distance check; never punch down; be most careful in text and with strangers. Discount the precise percentages (27 percent, 18 percent, 30 percent, age 23), which the Stage 1 files could not verify.

### 3.3 How far and how fast to disclose (top tension)
- **Ids:** `connect-02`, `how-to-know-a-person-02`, `talk-wood-brooks-03` versus `supercommunicators-08`, `supercommunicators-05`, `how-to-know-a-person-04`, `how-to-know-a-person-05`; boundaries in `connect-10`, `humor-seriously-05`, `communicate-with-mastery-08`.
- **The conflict:** Duhigg and David Brooks push toward depth and say vulnerability can come before trust; the research says people underestimate how welcome depth is. Connect prescribes small 15 percent stretches; Wood Brooks says not every conversation should go deep; David Brooks's own accompaniment chapter counsels patience. Who goes first also differs: Connect says the higher-status person should; Duhigg notes the junior person bears more risk.
- **Reconciliation:** Go one step deeper than feels comfortable, not ten. Make disclosure reciprocal and watch the response before the next step. Share feelings, uncertainties and learning edges, not doubts about your core competence. If you hold more power, go first. In front of an audience, disclose with a clear purpose and a planned beginning and end. The research on miscalibration is about acquaintances and strangers at moderate depth; it does not license oversharing, which every book warns against.

### 3.4 Apologize more versus apologize less
- **Ids:** `talk-wood-brooks-13`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-12` versus `communicate-with-mastery-17`, `improv-wisdom-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-02`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-04`, `communicate-with-mastery-14`, `humor-seriously-10`.
- **The conflict:** Wood Brooks says to apologize, even superfluously, because it signals empathy and builds trust. Schramm, Madson and Abrahams warn against apologizing for stumbles, pre-apologizing before a talk, or apologizing for an accent; Aaker and Bagdonas warn against over-apologizing for a flopped joke.
- **Reconciliation:** Apologize for effects on other people (harm, inconvenience, their bad luck). Do not apologize for your own performance (a slip, nerves, your accent, an unfunny line), which draws attention to it and signals low confidence. Wood Brooks's own mistakes list includes over-apologizing until it sounds like low confidence, so the books agree once the target is separated.

### 3.5 Imagine their perspective versus ask for it
- **Ids:** `how-to-know-a-person-11`, `talk-wood-brooks-12`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-16`, `made-to-stick-01` versus `how-to-know-a-person-06`, `how-to-know-a-person-14`, `connect-03`.
- **The conflict:** Several tools ask you to infer others' minds (mentalize from your own experience; plot your best guess at their goals; ask what would make their behavior reasonable). The perspective-getting research says imagining does not improve accuracy and can raise false confidence.
- **Reconciliation:** Treat any inference as a hypothesis to test by asking, not as knowledge. Imagining others' perspective is still useful for reducing egocentrism and generating charitable hypotheses (CLEAR's "explore their perspective"), as long as it is followed by a question and a loop.

### 3.6 Concise versus warm in writing
- **Ids:** `communicate-with-mastery-02`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-09` versus `supercommunicators-12`, `humor-seriously-13`.
- **The conflict:** Schramm and Abrahams say cut hard; Duhigg says overdo politeness, greetings and gratitude online; Aaker and Bagdonas say add small human touches.
- **Reconciliation:** Be brief about content and generous about tone. A one-line warm opener and a clear thanks cost little; padding the substance costs a lot. Duhigg's own mistake list warns against over-correcting into vague politeness that hides the request. Keep brevity from becoming curtness (Abrahams makes the same point).

### 3.7 Feedback as data versus feedback with consequences
- **Ids:** `connect-04`, `connect-08`, `connect-07` versus `think-faster-talk-smarter-10`.
- **The conflict:** Connect says feedback is data offered to open problem solving, not a verdict meant to force change. Abrahams's 4 I's ends with Implications, what follows if the person does or does not change.
- **Reconciliation:** Start with Connect's stance in peer and personal relationships. Add Abrahams's Implications when you have responsibility for the outcome (a manager, a repeated issue), stated as realistic consequences rather than threats, which Abrahams himself specifies. Both models share the behavior-plus-impact core and an invitation to talk.

### 3.8 Say yes versus set boundaries
- **Ids:** `improv-wisdom-01`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-05` versus `connect-13`, `connect-12`.
- **The conflict:** "Yes, and" encourages accepting offers; Connect encourages stating limits and rebalancing influence.
- **Reconciliation:** Madson herself separates accepting the reality of an offer from agreeing to it. Yes-and is for ideas and emotional bids (build on what was offered); boundaries are for commitments. "Yes, I hear the request, and here is what I can do" honors both. Abrahams makes the same distinction (building on the offer is not conceding the point).

### 3.9 Match emotion versus regulate your own
- **Ids:** `supercommunicators-02`, `supercommunicators-09` versus `think-faster-talk-smarter-16`, `communicate-with-mastery-13`.
- **The conflict:** Duhigg says match mood and energy; Abrahams says check yourself first in conflict; Schramm says slow and lower for weight.
- **Reconciliation:** Duhigg's own caution resolves it: match the type of conversation and acknowledge the emotion, but do not match escalation (anger, panic). Regulate yourself, then meet the other person in their kind of conversation.

### 3.10 Stories versus data
- **Ids:** `made-to-stick-09`, `made-to-stick-12` versus `made-to-stick-14`, `made-to-stick-08`.
- **The conflict:** Within Made to Stick, "one person, not a million" says statistics can dampen caring, while other chapters advise translating numbers to human scale and using statistics for credibility.
- **Reconciliation:** The identifiable-victim effect did not replicate in a pre-registered test (`made-to-stick-09`, Low). Pair a concrete human example with the key number, translated to human scale, rather than dropping data. Decision-makers still need the figure.

### 4.6 Gino integrity note (user decision: keep with note)
Francesca Gino coauthored three papers the library relies on: Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino (2017) on question-asking and liking; Yeomans, Minson, Collins, Chen and Gino (2020) on conversational receptiveness; and Collins, Dorison, Gino and Minson (2022) on underestimating learning goals.
- Directly cited in the book for: `talk-wood-brooks-04`, `talk-wood-brooks-05`, `talk-wood-brooks-09`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-16`.

- Recommendation: keep the insights and attach a standard one-line integrity note wherever these papers appear. More substantively, note that the question-asking finding has a separate methodological critique (Kluger and Malloy, 2019) and that both clusters have independent support (Sievers et al., 2024 for question-asking in groups; Ren and Schaumberg, 2024 for disagreement and listening), so neither concept depends solely on Gino-coauthored work.

## 6. Proposed narrative arc for the guide

Seven stages, 92 core insights. The rest of the library stays available as reference and cross-references. Selection favors High and Medium insights confirmed in the book, foundational ideas that later stages build on, and at most one insight per duplicate pair.

### Dependency logic in brief
- Stage 1 removes the two blockers that undermine every later skill: perfectionism and unmanaged anxiety. It ends by turning attention outward to audience and purpose.
- Stage 2 (listening) must precede Stage 3 (conversation), because good questions and disclosure depend on hearing the answer and diagnosing what kind of conversation the other person wants.
- Stage 3 builds rapport and trust that Stage 6 relies on: hard conversations go better inside relationships with some trust in the bank, and looping (Stage 2) is the core conflict tool.
- Stage 4 (clarity) depends on Stage 1's audience-first habit and is the base for Stage 5 (influence): you cannot persuade with a message no one understands.
- Stage 6 combines listening, disclosure of real feelings, structure and identity awareness, so it comes after all four earlier stages.
- Stage 7 integrates everything under pressure and in groups, and installs the practice loop that keeps skills improving.

### Stage 1: Foundations: a learnable skill, calm enough to connect
- **Purpose:** Replace "I am not a natural communicator" with a practice mindset; give the reader tools to manage nerves; establish that every communication starts with audience and purpose.
- **Categories:** foundations (mindset, anxiety, audience-goals, spontaneous-speaking).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `communicate-with-mastery-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-04`, `improv-wisdom-05`, `improv-wisdom-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-02`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-03`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-05`, `improv-wisdom-03`, `communicate-with-mastery-01`, `improv-wisdom-08` (10).
- **Why this order:** Start with the frame (mastery, not perfection) and its practical form (connection over perfection, be average, mistakes as offers). Then the body (calm), the label (excitement), the meaning (opportunity, not threat) and the behavior (act before you feel ready). Finish by pointing attention outward: AIM and keeping the purpose in view, which sets up the listening stage.

### Stage 2: Listening and understanding others
- **Purpose:** Build the habit of full, visible attention; correct overconfidence in reading people; teach the loop; diagnose what kind of conversation the other person is having.
- **Categories:** relationships (listening, empathy, nonverbal), foundations (mindset).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `improv-wisdom-06`, `how-to-know-a-person-03`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-06`, `how-to-know-a-person-14`, `how-to-know-a-person-06`, `how-to-know-a-person-07`, `connect-09`, `how-to-know-a-person-11`, `connect-03`, `supercommunicators-01`, `supercommunicators-02` (11).
- **Why this order:** Attention first (the High-confidence base), then making it visible, then listening for the bottom line. Next, humility: you read people less accurately than you think, so get their perspective rather than guess. That motivates looping as the verification tool and inquiry before advice. Empathy's three skills and the three realities give a model of the other mind and of intent versus impact, which later feedback skills need. End with the three kinds of conversations and matching, which turn listening into choosing how to respond.

### Stage 3: Conversation and connection
- **Purpose:** Move from small talk to real connection: questions, topics, depth, disclosure, levity, appreciation, and good endings.
- **Categories:** conversations (small-talk, humor, situations), relationships (question-design, rapport, trust-vulnerability, networking).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `talk-wood-brooks-01`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-13`, `talk-wood-brooks-04`, `talk-wood-brooks-06`, `talk-wood-brooks-02`, `talk-wood-brooks-03`, `how-to-know-a-person-04`, `supercommunicators-05`, `how-to-know-a-person-09`, `talk-wood-brooks-07`, `connect-02`, `supercommunicators-08`, `connect-10`, `talk-wood-brooks-08`, `humor-seriously-09`, `talk-wood-brooks-10`, `talk-wood-brooks-11` (17).
- **Why this order:** TALK gives the map. Curiosity and follow-ups are the easiest high-return change; boomerasking is the most common failure. Preparing topics and the pyramid then show how to climb from small talk. The depth evidence (High) and deep questions justify the climb; asking for stories and sensitive questions extend it. Disclosure comes next because depth is reciprocal: the 15 percent rule sets the pace, vulnerability invites trust, and dropping image management sustains it. Levity with a safety check keeps things light, appreciation strengthens the bond, and knowing when to end closes the stage.

### Stage 4: Clarity and structure in speech and writing
- **Purpose:** Make messages easy to understand: beat the curse of knowledge, lead with the core, use a reusable structure, make it concrete, and adapt for text.
- **Categories:** written-digital (clarity-structure, email, executive-writing, tone-misreading, chat), foundations (message-structures), professional (status-updates, executive-communication).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `made-to-stick-01`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-09`, `made-to-stick-03`, `communicate-with-mastery-05`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-07`, `made-to-stick-07`, `made-to-stick-04`, `made-to-stick-14`, `communicate-with-mastery-02`, `communicate-with-mastery-09`, `supercommunicators-12` (11).
- **Why this order:** The curse of knowledge explains why clarity is hard, building on AIM from Stage 1. Focus and finding the core decide what to say; parachuting in decides where to start; What, So What, Now What gives the shape. Concreteness (High), analogies and human-scale numbers make the content land. The written block then applies all of it to text: ABC editing, pause-verify-iterate (because senders overestimate how well tone comes across), and extra warmth and explicitness online.

### Stage 5: Influence and memorable messages
- **Purpose:** Move people to act: lead with their benefit, use persuasive structures, make ideas stick, build credibility, and work with identity rather than against it.
- **Categories:** influence (persuasive-structure, memorable-messages, credibility, framing, overcoming-resistance), professional (managing-up, negotiation).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `made-to-stick-10`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-08`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-15`, `communicate-with-mastery-18`, `made-to-stick-02`, `made-to-stick-05`, `made-to-stick-06`, `made-to-stick-12`, `humor-seriously-16`, `made-to-stick-08`, `made-to-stick-13`, `supercommunicators-10`, `made-to-stick-11` (13).
- **Why this order:** Benefit-first persuasion extends audience thinking. The structures (Problem Solution Benefit, the pitch starters, the fuller pitch) come before stickiness so the reader has a skeleton to strengthen. SUCCESs then organizes the stickiness tools (unexpected, curiosity, stories, a light line). Credibility and emotional spread come next, and identity closes the stage because it explains why good arguments still fail and bridges into difficult conversations.

### Stage 6: Difficult situations
- **Purpose:** Handle feedback, conflict, apologies, boundaries and distress without damaging the relationship.
- **Categories:** difficult (giving-feedback, receiving-feedback, conflict, apologies, boundaries, saying-no, situations), personal-life (repair, partners, family, supporting-distress).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `connect-07`, `connect-05`, `connect-06`, `connect-04`, `connect-15`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-10`, `connect-08`, `talk-wood-brooks-14`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-16`, `talk-wood-brooks-09`, `supercommunicators-11`, `supercommunicators-14`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-12`, `talk-wood-brooks-13`, `connect-14`, `connect-13`, `how-to-know-a-person-10` (17).
- **Why this order:** Receiving feedback comes first: it is the reader's own side, and it establishes why others' data matters. Raising pinches early then sets the timing; real feelings, behavior plus impact and behavior-not-personality build the message; the 4 I's packages it; feedback as problem solving sets the stance. Conflict follows, starting with diagnosis (find the layer), then regulation and receptiveness (CLEAR, receptiveness), then group-level safety and norms. Repair comes after conflict (apologize specifically, apologize for others' bad luck, ruptures can be repaired). Boundaries and supporting someone in distress close the stage because they draw on everything before.

### Stage 7: Mastery: groups, high stakes, and deliberate practice
- **Purpose:** Integrate the skills under pressure and in front of groups, lead conversations that elevate others, and set up a lasting practice loop.
- **Categories:** public-speaking (impromptu, qa, delivery, structure), conversations (group-dynamics, situations), professional (meetings), foundations (spontaneous-speaking).
- **Core insights (ordered):** `think-faster-talk-smarter-01`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-11`, `improv-wisdom-13`, `think-faster-talk-smarter-14`, `communicate-with-mastery-06`, `communicate-with-mastery-17`, `communicate-with-mastery-11`, `supercommunicators-06`, `improv-wisdom-11`, `humor-seriously-14`, `communicate-with-mastery-16`, `communicate-with-mastery-20`, `communicate-with-mastery-15` (13).
- **Why this order:** The six-step method recaps Stages 1, 2 and 4 as one sequence for on-the-spot speaking, then ADD, speaking from prompts and the toast apply it. Delivery (one thought one person, verbal vocal visual, slides) follows once content is in place; the contested elements of delivery are flagged. Group leadership (ask more in groups, make your partner look good, set the levity culture) extends Stage 3 to teams. The canvas is the high-stakes integration of AIM. The stage, and the guide, ends with the practice loop: video self-review and coaching, so learning continues after the guide.
